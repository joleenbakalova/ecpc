# Early Childhood Development and Its Promise for Peace
## Research Brief — 2026 Evidence Update

*Prepared on behalf of the ECPC Programmatic and Policy & Advocacy Working Groups*
*July 2026*

> This brief updates the September 2023 UNGA-78 Policy Briefing Note *“Early Childhood Development and Its Promise for Peace”* (Hanöz-Penney, 2023). It draws on a curated repository of **103 studies by 25 ECPC-affiliated researchers, published 2023–2026** (92 peer-reviewed journal articles and 11 grey-literature reports, briefs, and book chapters), each coded from its full text. The evidence is organised to show how the case for early childhood development (ECD) as a strategy for peace has *strengthened, sharpened, and scaled* since UNGA-78.

---

## Why an update now

At UNGA-78, the ECPC argued that investing in ECD is a powerful, cost-effective strategy for reducing violence and building peaceful societies, and it called for more research — "**especially in countries experiencing crisis and conflict.**" That research has since arrived. In the three years since, ECPC members have delivered randomised trials in refugee camps and conflict-affected states, cost-effectiveness analyses, multi-generation biological studies, and global financing analyses. This brief distils what is new and what it means for the ECPC's asks.

**The headline:** we no longer need to argue only from *promise*. We can now show, with trial-grade evidence from the settings that matter most, that ECD services reduce violence in the home, reach the hardest-to-reach families, and can be delivered affordably at government scale — while a widening financing gap threatens to leave conflict-affected children furthest behind.

---

## The Challenge (updated)

Conflict is still rising, and children still bear its heaviest burden — but we can now quantify that burden more precisely, and trace its biology.

- **Scale.** Roughly **1 in 5 children worldwide now live in a conflict-affected area — at least 224 million school-age children in 2023** (Aber et al., 2025); an estimated **473 million children live in fragile and conflict-affected settings** (Betancourt et al., 2026).
- **Violence begins at home, and it is near-universal.** **Two of every three children under five in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) experience corporal punishment** (Cuartas, 2023). New multi-country evidence links that violence directly to lost development: across four LMIC cohorts, spanking predicted lower numeracy (β = −0.23), literacy (β = −0.24), social-emotional (β = −0.22) and motor skills (β = −0.23), with **no evidence of any positive outcome** (Cuartas et al., 2024).
- **Caregivers are in crisis too.** In a cohort of 1,133 caregivers in conflict-affected Colombia, **44.7% screened positive for depression and 24.5% for PTSD** (Ponguta et al., 2024) — a reminder that a child's most important protective relationship is itself under strain.
- **The damage is biologically embedded — and intergenerational.** A landmark three-generation, epigenome-wide study of Syrian refugee families found a **common epigenetic signature of violence transmitted across germline, prenatal, and direct exposure**, alongside accelerated epigenetic ageing (Panter-Brick et al., 2025). This is the clearest evidence yet for the "intergenerational cycles" the ECPC was founded to break — and for intervening early, before those cycles close.
- **The money is not following the need.** Development assistance for early childhood care and education (ECCE) is just **1.7% of all education aid — far below UNICEF's suggested 10% minimum** — and **conflict-affected countries receive only US$0.3 per child per year, a quarter of the US$1.2 received by non-conflict countries** (Cuartas et al., 2024). Meanwhile **less than 1% of development assistance is allocated to mental health** (Betancourt et al., 2026). The children at greatest risk get the least investment.

## What the New Evidence Shows

### 1. ECD services reduce violence in the home — and work at government scale
The most important advance since UNGA-78 is evidence of impact *delivered through government systems, at scale*. In Rwanda, the **Sugira Muryango** home-visiting programme — delivered by government child-protection volunteers — **reduced harsh discipline (OR = 0.34)**, increased father involvement (IRR = 1.18), and improved home stimulation, learning materials, and dietary diversity (Cohen's *d* = 0.20–0.37) across 538 households (Black et al., 2025). Crucially, it is affordable: the programme cost **US$456 per family, projected to fall below US$200 when scaled through government systems** (Betancourt et al., 2023). This is a working template for the ECPC's goal of reaching 200 million children.

### 2. Interventions now reach the hardest-to-reach families — remotely and through non-specialists
Distance, cost, and a shortage of specialists no longer have to be barriers.
- **By phone:** A cluster-RCT with **2,298 Syrian and Jordanian refugee caregivers**, delivered *audio-only* by community health volunteers, produced a small but robust reduction in caregiver depression (ES = −0.11) (Yoshikawa et al., 2024).
- **By non-specialists:** In Sierra Leone, a family intervention delivered by community health workers significantly improved observed caregiver–child interaction and **home responsiveness (+1.66, p < .001)**, and trended toward less intimate-partner violence (Betancourt et al., 2024).
- **By digital tools:** In rural Peru, an AI-supported parenting chatbot matched home visiting **at one-fifteenth of the cost**, with superior cost-effectiveness (Cuartas et al., 2026).

Together these show a delivery revolution: nurturing-care support can now be brought to displaced and remote families at a fraction of historical cost.

### 3. The whole family matters — especially fathers
New evidence moves decisively beyond the mother-and-child dyad.
- Among Syrian refugee families, it was not the *level* of father involvement but **spousal disagreement about it** that predicted worse outcomes — children's social-emotional learning was more than **1 standard deviation lower** in the most discordant families (Panter-Brick et al., 2024).
- A cluster-RCT with **2,002 fathers** in Rohingya camps and host communities showed that adding a father-engagement component improved fathers' parenting, family engagement, and child social-emotional development (Yoshikawa et al., 2026).
- For resettled refugee families in the US, a family-strengthening intervention **reduced children's conduct problems (β = −9.20, p = .01)** and improved parental supervision (Black et al., 2025).

### 4. Social-emotional learning builds self-regulation in education-in-emergencies
SEL is now among the best-tested ECD strategies in crisis settings.
- In **Niger**, adding a mindfulness component to remedial tutoring **reduced children's sadness dysregulation (ES = −0.30)**, with refugees and girls benefiting most (Aber et al., 2026).
- In **Lebanon**, 26 weeks of Healing Classroom Tutoring improved externally-observed behavioral regulation (ES = 0.31) — though longer dosage was *not* uniformly better, a valuable design lesson for implementers (Aber et al., 2023).
- A meta-analysis of **90 studies (20,626 students)** confirms universal school-based SEL improves academic, behavioral, and social-emotional outcomes and reduces distress — strongest when teachers deliver it (McCarthy et al., 2024).

### 5. The evidence base is consolidating — with one critical gap
- A systematic review of **80 studies (n = 18,193)** in LMIC and humanitarian settings found **43 showed benefits to child or adolescent mental health and only 3 found none** — with caregiver psychoeducation, communication skills, non-specialist delivery, and father engagement as the recurring active ingredients (Betancourt et al., 2024).
- A network meta-analysis of **59 RCTs (18,349 children under four)** found relationship-focused parenting interventions reduced child internalising problems (SMD = −0.26), and that **children left on a waitlist got worse** — evidence against delay (BMJ Mental Health, 2023).
- **But the research is not being produced where it is needed.** A scoping review of 6,843 records found only six studies of digital mental-health tools for war- and disaster-affected youth, and **fewer than 10% of relevant interventions were developed in the LMICs where war and displacement actually occur** (Leckman et al., 2024). Closing this gap is the single clearest research priority.

### 6. The economic case — and why cash alone is not enough
Failing to invest in the "next 1,000 days" (ages 2–5) carries large human-capital and economic costs; a minimum package such as one year of ECCE for every child is costly but worthwhile (Cuartas et al., 2024, *Lancet*). At the same time, rigorous US trials of unconditional cash transfers (Baby's First Years) found that **monthly cash alone produced no detectable gains in child development or maternal well-being** (Yoshikawa et al., 2023, 2024, 2025). The lesson for financing is clear: **income support must be paired with nurturing-care and ECD services** — money is necessary but not sufficient.

## Key Messages (2026)

- **ECD reduces violence and builds peace — now with trial evidence from conflict settings.** Home-visiting and family programmes measurably cut harsh discipline and intimate-partner violence and strengthen the caregiver–child bond in Rwanda, Sierra Leone, Jordan, and Bangladesh.
- **It can be delivered affordably, at scale, through existing systems** — government volunteers, community health workers, phones, and even AI chatbots — at as little as one-fifteenth of historical cost.
- **The whole ecosystem matters.** Engaging fathers and reducing family conflict, not just supporting mothers, drives child outcomes.
- **The harm of inaction is now visible in children's biology** and passes across generations — making early action urgent, not optional.
- **The financing gap is the binding constraint.** Conflict-affected children receive a quarter of the already-inadequate ECCE aid other children get, and child mental health receives under 1% of development assistance.
- **Cash alone is not enough** — investment must fund *services*, not transfers in isolation.

## Key Highlights

- **473 million** children live in fragile and conflict-affected settings; **~1 in 5 children** globally live in conflict-affected areas (Betancourt et al., 2026; Aber et al., 2025).
- **2 of 3** children under five in LMICs experience corporal punishment (Cuartas, 2023).
- Harsh discipline fell by roughly two-thirds (**OR = 0.34**) under a government-delivered home-visiting programme in Rwanda (Black et al., 2025).
- A refugee parenting programme reached **2,298 caregivers by phone** and lowered caregiver depression (Yoshikawa et al., 2024).
- A digital parenting tool matched home visits at **1/15 the cost** (Cuartas et al., 2026).
- **43 of 80** interventions in LMIC/humanitarian settings improved child mental health; only **3** showed no effect (Betancourt et al., 2024).
- Conflict-affected countries receive just **US$0.3 per child** in ECCE aid — a quarter of the amount non-conflict countries receive (Cuartas et al., 2024).
- **<10%** of digital mental-health interventions for war-affected youth are developed in the LMICs where war occurs (Leckman et al., 2024).

## What the ECPC is Asking For

Building on the 2023 asks, and sharpened by the new evidence:

1. **Recognition.** A UN General Assembly Resolution on ECD and Peacebuilding. The evidence base is now mature enough — trial-grade, from conflict settings — to anchor a formal commitment.

2. **Support for Policy and Advocacy.** Public–private partnerships to operationalise ECD-for-peace policy. Priority: embed proven programmes (e.g., government-delivered home visiting) into national child-protection and education systems, where costs fall and reach grows.

3. **Support for Research — with a geographic correction.** Fewer than 10% of relevant interventions are developed where war actually occurs. New investment must fund **locally-developed research in crisis-affected LMICs**, including social-cohesion and peacebuilding outcomes and long-term follow-up.

4. **Support for ECD Services that Promote Peacebuilding.** Scale comprehensive, multi-level services toward 200 million children — leveraging the new low-cost delivery models (non-specialist, remote, and digital) that make this target realistic.

5. **Partnerships and Financing.** The binding constraint is money. We ask partners and donors to **raise ECCE's share of education aid toward the 10% minimum and to protect conflict-affected children's share**, and to fund *services*, not cash transfers alone.

## About this evidence update

This brief synthesises the **ECPC research repository** — 103 studies by 25 ECPC-affiliated researchers (2023–2026), each coded from its full text against a 110-field research schema. Flagship findings were selected using a transparent relevance ranking that weights alignment with the ECPC "Peacebuilding through ECD" Theory of Change and crisis-context relevance (50%), evidence rigour (30%), and citation impact among peer-reviewed work (20%); reviews and syntheses without original data are not penalised. The full repository, a top-15 shortlist with the most quotable statistic from each study, and per-study source PDFs are available to the Working Groups (`07072026ECPCRR.csv`, `ADVOCACY_SHORTLIST.md`, `pipeline/ECPC_Repository_for_review.xlsx`).

*Two evidence notes: the network-meta-analysis finding (§5) is drawn from a 2023 BMJ Mental Health study held in the repository under review for author attribution; the Baby's First Years cash-transfer trials (§6) are US-based and are cited only for the "cash-alone-is-insufficient" argument, not as conflict-setting evidence.*
